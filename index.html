Bio

I currently work at Facebook AI Applied Research, where I solve AI problems that lie at the intersection of research and product. I am particularly interested in Natural Language Processing (NLP), a subset of machine learning that focuses on language. Previously, I got my bachelors in computer science from the University of Washington, where I did nearly 2 years of undergraduate NLP research with Prof. Luke Zettlemoyer (as well as Eunsol Choi and Gabriel Stanovsky). Before UW, I “skipped high school” through the Robinson Center’s Transition School/Early Entrance Program.

Research Interest

Broadly, I am interested in developing systems that can understand language in a robust and generalizable manner. Specifically, there are a few problems that have emerged in the field recently:

  • Generalizability: Can we make NLP models extrapolate to arbitrary, possibly unseen, tasks?
  • Interpretability: Current deep learning models, esp. state-of-the-art ones in NLP, are often described as “black boxes.” Can we make them more transparent? And more importantly, without sacrificing accuracy?
  • Scalability and resource efficiency: Current models are extremely costly to train, and have significant downstream environmental and economic impacts. How can we mitigate these issue?
  • AI for social good: Reducing social bias in NLP models (see above paragraphs), misinformation or fake news detection, etc.

In the past, I have worked on projects in topics such as coreference resolution, fake news detection, and question-answering.

To speak more abstractly, I credit my original fascination with language to learning about post-structural/post-Sassurean theory (Derrida, Barthes, etc.) in Transition School, and in particular, reading Catherine Belsey’s introduction to the topic, Critical Practice. While I won’t claim to have understood these theories 100%, regardless, learning about them has fundamentally changed my outlook and understanding of language. They led to my current belief that language is absolutely foundational to both our society, and the way we think: language is an imperfect medium, imbued with social ideologies, power structures, and oppressive norms, through which we think, reason, and understand the world around us. Thus, I believe so-called “teaching machines to read and write” is probably one of the most intriguing, difficult, and potentially problematic (if we are not careful) challenges of the 21st century. Moreover, as our own individual psychology and thinking itself is molded and constrained by language (in particular the categories, or “systems of differences” defined by language), I believe approaching AI through language will be the main driving force towards artificial general intelligence (if we will ever be able to achieve that).
